###### Reflection (Mirror) Symmetry - occurs when one half of an object or shape is a

mirror image of the other half. If you can draw a line down the middle (called the line of symmetry) and both sides match exactly, then the shape has reflection symmetry. It's like folding a picture in half and having both sides line up perfectly!

###### Rotational Symmetry - designs that repeat around a central point

###### Key Parts of Rotational Symmetry: Center of rotation - the spot the shape rotates around.

**Angle of rotation - how far you turn the shape each time it looks the same. Order of symmetry - how many times the shape matches itself in one full turn.**

###### The importance of symmetries in nature and art in terms of:

###### 1. Reflection Symmetry (Mirror Symmetry)

***Nature: Many living organisms exhibit mirror symmetry. For example, butterflies***

and human bodies are bilaterally symmetrical-one side is a mirror image of the other. This symmetry is often linked to biological function and evolutionary advantages like streamlined movement or balanced sensory input.

***Art: In visual art and architecture, mirror symmetry is used to create harmony and***

a sense of order. Traditional Islamic art, for instance, heavily relies on reflection symmetry to produce visually pleasing patterns.

###### 2. Rotational Symmetry

###### Nature: Flowers, starfish, and snowflakes often show rotational symmetry. For

instance, a five-petaled flower has fivefold rotational symmetry. This type of symmetry helps in maximizing exposure to sunlight or efficient reproduction.

***Arts: Mandalas and circular designs use rotational symmetry to evoke balance and***

spirituality. Artists use it to guide the viewer's eye around a central point, creating unity in the composition.

###### 3. Line of Symmetry

***Nature: A line of symmetry divides an object into two identical parts. Leaves, insects,***

and many animals possess a vertical line of symmetry. It often reflects functional optimization, like flight in birds or balance in humans.

***Arts: Lines of symmetry are used to establish focal points or create balanced***

compositions. A portrait may use a vertical line of symmetry for a formal and composed look.

***Nature: The center of a sunflower or a jellyfish is a clear example of where rotational***

symmetry originates. It helps distribute resources evenly (like seeds in a sunflower).

***Art: In decorative art, like rose windows in Gothic cathedrals, the center of rotation***

is often the visual focus, radiating symmetry outward to engage the viewer.

Nature: The angle of rotation defines how many times an object maps onto itself in a full 360° turn. For example, a snowflake typically has 60° rotational symmetry (sixfold). Art: In pattern design or tessellations, the angle of rotation defines how elements repeat and interlock, essential for creating intricate and mesmerizing visuals.

| Key Ideas and | Dilation enlarges or reduces the original figure proportionally from a fixed center, |
|---|---|
| Examples | called the center of dilation. The scale factor k determines the change |

The center of dilation is a fixed point in the plane about which all points are expanded or contracted. "How different is one line from the other in the above figure?"

The scale factor refers to how much the figure grows or shrinks, and it is denoted by:

a. If, the image gets smaller and closer to the center of dilation.
b. If, the image gets larger and farther from the center of dilation.

###### Vector Rule: CP' = k · CP

**General Coordinate Formula: P'(x', y') = (a + k(x - a), b + k(y - b))** [Center C = (a, b)] **Origin Coordinate Formula: P'(x', y') = (kx, ky)** [Center C = (0, 0)]

When a shape or segment is dilated from the origin, every coordinate pair (x, y) is multiplied directly by the scale factor k to produce the new mapped image points:

###### Coordinate Mapping Rule: (x, y) → (kx, ky)

| Key Ideas and Examples | A dilation is a transformation that changes the size of a figure but not its shape. It is defined by a center of dilation (C') and a scale factor (k). The key rule is that the position of the new point P' is determined by the vector relationship: CP' = k•CP This means P' lies on the line passing through C and P. The distance from C to P' is /k/ times the distance from C to P . Our illustration provides three distinct scenarios using the same initial setup: Reduction (0 < k < ): When the magnitude of is between 0 and 1, the points move closer to the center. As shown in the left panel (k = 0.5), the entire figure shrinks by half. Unchanged (k = 1): When the scale factor is exactly 1, the mapping CP' means every point P' lands exactly on its original point P. The figure is invariant. Enlargement (k > 1): When k is greater than 1, the points move further away from the center. In the right panel (k = 1.5), the entire figure grows by 50%. The new points P' are 1.5 times farther from C than the original points P . |

| Key Ideas and Examples: | Tessellation-a repetition of geometric figures on a two-dimensional plane that has no overlaps or gaps. What tessellates? |

-----

When we say that a polygon tessellates the plane, we mean that:

- Copies of the polygon can be repeated endlessly
- They fit together perfectly
- They leave no empty spaces and do not overlap.

###### Important rule:

If the angles add up to more than 360, the shapes will overlap. If the angles add up to less than 360, gaps will appear.

###### Translation means sliding a shape from one position to another without turning

or flipping it.

- The shape keeps the same orientation.
- All points of the shape move the same distance in the same direction.

###### Rotation means turning a shape around a fixed point called the center of

rotation.

- The shape turns by a certain angle(e.g., 60̊, 90̊, 120̊)
- The shape remains congruent to the original.

###### Reflection means flipping a shape over a line, creating a mirror image. The

reflected shape is reversed but remains the same size and shape.

###### Tessellating Polygons

A square has an interior angle of 90°. At a vertex:

**90° + 90° + 90° + 90° = 360°**

✔ No gaps ✔ No overlaps

**Transformation Used: Translation (sliding the square repeatedly)**

![](img_p22_2.png)

An equilateral triangle has an interior angle of 60°. At a vertex:

**60° × 6 = 360°**

✔ Six triangles meet perfectly

###### Transformations Used:

Rotation + Translation

![](img_p22_1.png)

**120° + 120° + 120° = 360°**

✔ Common in nature (e.g., honeycomb pattern)

###### Transformation Used: Translation

| Key Ideas | What is an Escher-Type Tessellation? |
|---|---|
| and | An Escher-type tessellation is an artistic, interlocking tiling where a regular polygon |
| Examples: | (such as a square, equilateral triangle, or hexagon) is modified using geometric transformations to create recognizable shapes like birds, fish, reptiles, or human |

figures. Named after the famous Dutch graphic artist M.C. Escher, these designs rely on a strict mathematical rule: whatever area you remove from one side of a tile, you must add back to another side. Because the total surface area remains perfectly invariant, the final stylized figures fit together flawlessly with zero gaps and zero overlaps. Three Fundamental Rules of Escher Tilings:

1. Conservation of Area: The area of the final stylized tile must exactly equal the area of the base polygon template.
2. Isometry (Rigid Motion): Transformations must preserve lengths and angle measures to ensure a seamless fit.
3. Vertex Sums: The interior angles of the shapes meeting at any given intersection point must always sum up to precisely 360°.

Geometric Transformation Methods Artists and mathematicians manipulate polygon boundaries using three main types of rigid geometric transformations to generate an endless variety of interlocking figures.

1. Translation Tessellations (The 'Nibble and Slide' Method) This technique typically starts with a square or a rectangle. You alter one side of the polygon by cutting out a unique boundary curve, then slide (translate) that exact modification directly across to the opposite parallel side where it is appended. Vector Mapping Rule: T\_⟨w, 0⟩(x, y) = (x + w, y) (where 'w' is the width of the baseline template square).
2. Rotation Tessellations (The 'Pivot' Method) Instead of sliding a cutout across to an opposite side, a rotation tessellation involves pivoting a modification around a shared vertex point (a corner) onto an adjacent side. This technique is heavily utilized with equilateral triangles (rotating 60°), squares (rotating 90°), and regular hexagons (rotating 120°). It often produces beautiful spiral arrangements of figures.
3. Reflection & Glide Reflection Tessellations A glide reflection combines a reflection over an axis line with a parallel translation (slide). Because a reflection flips the orientation of the shape, every alternating

-----

column or row of the tessellation will look like a mirror image ('left-handed' versus 'right-handed' versions of the same figure). This creates dynamic patterns where shapes appear to be marching or flying in alternating directions. ###### 1. The Golden Ratio in Nature

In nature, the Golden Ratio is less about a "mystical blueprint" and more about efficiency. It is closely tied to the Fibonacci Sequence (1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13…), where dividing any number by its predecessor gets closer and closer to 1.618 as the sequence progresses.

Nature utilizes this mathematical pattern to maximize space and energy consumption:

- Phyllotaxis (Leaf and Seed Arrangement): Look closely at a sunflower head or a pinecone. The seeds grow in clockwise and counterclockwise spirals. The number of spirals almost always equals two consecutive Fibonacci numbers (e.g., 34 and 55 spirals). This exact spacing, dictated by , ensures that every seed gets maximum sunlight and room to grow without crowding out its neighbor.
- Shells and Marine Life: The chambered nautilus shell grows wider at a constant geometric rate, closely mimicking the logarithmic expansion of the Golden Spiral.

This allows the organism to grow larger without changing its fundamental shape or losing structural integrity.

- Hurricanes and Galaxies: On a grand scale, the spiral arms of a spinning hurricane and the vast swirling arms of spiral galaxies (like our Milky Way) naturally arrange themselves along a logarithmic spiral close to , matching the path of least resistance for moving matter and energy.

###### 2. The Golden Ratio in Art and Architecture

Artists and architects throughout history have gravitated toward the Golden Ratio because the human eye processes images composed around it remarkably fast, subconsciously associating it with balance and beauty.

###### Classical Architecture The Parthenon (Ancient Greece): While historians debate whether the Greeks used

the ratio explicitly, a Golden Rectangle fits almost perfectly around the facade of the Parthenon. The spacing of its columns and the height of its structural elements heavily mirror these proportions.

###### Renaissance Masterpieces Leonardo da Vinci: Da Vinci was fascinated by mathematics and illustrated a book

titled De Divina Proportione ("On the Divine Proportion"). He intentionally incorporated the ratio into his masterpieces:

- The Mona Lisa: If you draw a Golden Rectangle over her face and a Golden Spiral starting from her wrist, it contours perfectly around her eye and face, guiding the viewer's gaze.
- The Last Supper: The architectural layout of the room, the alignment of the windows, and the positioning of Jesus and the disciples are mapped out using these geometric constraints.
- Modern Art and Design
- Salvador Dalí: In his painting The Sacrament of the Last Supper, Dalí explicitly painted the canvas to fit the exact dimensions of a Golden Rectangle. He also chose a massive, translucent regular dodecahedron (a 3D shape built from pentagons, which are deeply tied to the Golden Ratio) to float above the table.

###### Modern Branding: You see it every day in graphic design. Corporate giants

like Apple, Google (in its G logo), and Twitter/X use intersecting circles derived from the Golden Ratio to construct their iconic logos, ensuring visual harmony and instantly recognizable symmetry.

###### Components of a Matrix

- **Element - each individual number or symbol in a matrix.**
- **Rows - horizontal lines of elements.**
- **Columns - vertical lines of elements.**
- **Dimension - indicates how many rows and columns the matrix has. The**

**dimension of a matrix is written as i × j, where i is the number of rows**

and j is the number of columns.

###### Operations of Matrices

###### 1. Matrix addition is performed by adding the corresponding elements of two

matrices that have the same dimension (same number of rows and columns).

**Rule: If A=[aij]and B=bij are matrices of the same dimension, then**

A+B=aij+ [bij]

**Take note: Matrices must have the same dimensions to be added.**

###### 2. Matrix subtraction is performed by subtracting the corresponding

**elements of two matrices with the same dimension.**

**Rule: If A=[aij]and B=bij are matrices of the same dimension, then**

A-B=aij- [bij]

**Take note: Matrices must have the same dimensions to be subtracted.**

###### 3. Matrix Multiplication is the process of multiplying two matrices to get a new

matrix. For two matrices A and B to be multiplied, the number of columns in A must be equal to the number of rows in B.

**Rule: C = A × B where each element cij of C is calculated as:**

**cij = (row i of A) × (column j of B)**

###### Part 2. Linear Programming Model Construction

###### 1. Identify the Decision Variables

- Decision variables are the unknown quantities that we want to determine. They represent the choices available in the problem.
- Each variable must represent a measurable quantity.
- Variables must be clearly and simply defined.
- They are usually represented by x, y (or more variables if needed), but the

description of what they represent is more important than the symbol.

###### 2. Write the Objective Function

The objective function expresses what we want to maximize or minimize. It is based on the goal of the problem. There are two types:

- **Maximization = e.g., profit, output, revenue, efficiency**
- **Minimization = e.g., cost, time, waste**

###### How to write it:

1. Multiply each decision variable by its contribution to the objective.
2. Add them together to create the objective function.

###### 1. Write the Constraints

Constraints represent the limitations in the problem. *These may refer to:*

- Available resources (materials, time, labor, land, budget)
- Maximum production limits
- Storage, safety, or capacity restrictions
- Demand limits

Constraints must be expressed as linear inequalities or equations, commonly:

- "At most" → ≤
- "At least" → ≥
- "No more than" → ≤
- "No less than" → ≥
- "Exactly" → =

###### How to write constraints:

1. Identify the resource being limited.
2. Determine how much of that resource each product uses.
3. Combine them into an inequality.

###### 4. Add the Non-Negativity Restrictions

In real life, we cannot produce negative amounts. Therefore, all variables must be restricted:

**x ≥ 0 y ≥ 0 Key notes:**

- This ensures the solution stays realistic.

-----

- This condition is ALWAYS present in every LP problem.

###### 5. Combine All Parts into a Full LP Model

After identifying variables, objective function, constraints, and non-negativity, we group them logically into one clear model.

**Lesson 4.3. SOLVING LINEAR PROGRAMMING PROBLEMS USING SIMPLEX METHOD**

**Objectives:** By the end of the lesson, you are expected to:

1. identify and set up a linear program in standard maximization form;
2. convert inequality constraints to equations using slack variables;
3. set up the initial simplex tableau by performing pivoting operations; and
4. identify the optimal solution from the optimal simplex tableau

**Key Ideas: Scope and Conditions of the Simplex Method**

1. The LP must be a MAXIMIZATION problem (we maximize Z = c 1 x1 + c2 x2 + ... + ck xk).
2. Every structural constraint must be of the form a x 1 + b x2 + ... ≤ r, with the righthand side r non-negative.
3. Every decision variable must satisfy non-negativity: x1, x2, ..., xk ≥ 0.
4. There is one slack variable per ≤ constraint, so the slack variables form the initial basis with values equal to the RHS of each constraint.

###### Steps in Solving Linear Programming Using Simplex Method

1. Set up the problem.
2. Introduce slack variables.
3. Set up the initial simplex tableau.
4. Identify the entering variable.
5. Identify the leaving variable.
6. Normalize the pivot row by dividing the s1 row by 6.
7. Eliminate the other entries in the x3 column.
8. Repeat the entering-variable test.
9. Identify the leaving variable.
10. Pivot and eliminate the other x1-column entries.
11. Check optimality and read the solution.

**Examples: Solve using the Simplex method.**

Maximize: Z = 10x1 + 15x2 + 20x3 Subject to constraints: 2x1 + 4x2 + 6x3 ≤ 24 3x1 + 9x2 + 6x3 ≤ 30 x1, x2, x3 ≥ 0

2x1 + 4x2 + 6x3 + s1 = 24 3x1 + 9x2 + 6x3 + s2 = 30 Z - 10x1 - 15x2 - 20x3 = 0

x1, x2, x3, s1, s2 ≥ 0

| s2 | 3 | 9 | 6 | 0 | 1 | 30 |
| Z | -10 | -15 | -20 | 0 | 0 | 0 |
