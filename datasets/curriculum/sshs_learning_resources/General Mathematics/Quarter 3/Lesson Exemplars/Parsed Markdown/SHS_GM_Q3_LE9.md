A random variable is a variable whose value is determined by chance. It may take on numerical or categorical (nominal) values, depending on the experiment. Random variables are usually denoted by capital letters such as X, Y, or Z, and they represent the set of possible outcomes of a random experiment.

###### Graph of a Probability Distribution of a Discrete Random Variable

A graph of a probability distribution for a discrete random variable is a probability histogram or a stick

**graph (vertical line graph) that shows each possible value**

-----

###### How to Graph a Discrete Probability Distribution

1. List the values of the random variable (e.g., 0, 1, 2, 3 heads).
2. List the probability of each value.
3. Draw the x-axis labeled with the values of the random variable.
4. Draw the y-axis labeled with "Probability".
5. Draw bars or vertical lines whose heights correspond to the probability of each value.
6. Make sure the bars do not touch, since the data is discrete.

###### Expected Value

The expected value or mean (denoted as E(X) or µ) of a random variable X is the average or mean value that X will take over many repetitions of an experiment. It represents the long-run average outcome.

###### Expected value of Discrete Random Variable

For a discrete random variable with values 1, 2,,3, … , and probabilities 1, 2,,3, … ,

4. The area between two points is probability that X falls in that interval.

###### The Random Sampling

Random sampling means that every individual in the population has an equal chance of being selected. This method is important because it:

a. Reduces bias: No one is unfairly favored or left out.
b. Increases representativeness: The sample better reflects the diversity of the whole population.
c. Improves reliability of results: Conclusions drawn from a random sample are more likely to be valid for the whole population.

Example: If you have 500 learners and want to select 50 randomly, you might use a computer to generate 50 random numbers representing learner IDs.

###### Types of Sampling

###### Probability Sampling

Every individual has a known chance of being chosen. Probability sampling is generally preferred because it leads to more accurate and generalizable results, while nonprobability sampling can lead to bias.

Examples include: a. **Simple Random Sampling:**

-----

Everyone is equally likely to be picked (e.g. randomly drawing names or using a random number generator).

For example, in a population of 200 Grade 11 learners, the researcher needs a sample of 40 learners. Using simple random sampling, every learner in the population has an equal chance of being chosen. The researcher assigns numbers from 1 to 200 to all learners and then uses a random method, such as drawing lots or using a random number generator, to select 40 unique numbers. The learners correspond to those numbers form the sample.

Population Sample

![](img_p73_1.png)

b. Systematic Sampling:

Selecting every kth individual from a list (e.g., every 4th learner). The starting point is chosen at random.

For example, in 200 Grade 12 learners, you will choose 50 learners as members of your sample. To select which learners will be part of the sample, divide the population size 200 by the sample size 50, which results to 4. That means every 4th learner among the 200 Grade 12 learners will be included in the sample. You may also start on any randomly selected number from 1 to 4; for instance, if the starting number is 2, then the sample will include

-----

learners numbered 2, 6, 10, and so on, until the desired 50 learners are chosen. (Note: There are other patterns.)

Population Sample

![](img_p74_1.png)

c. Stratified Sampling:

The population is divided into subgroups or strata (e.g., by grade level or strand). Samples are randomly selected from each stratum to ensure all groups are represented.

For example, in a population of 300 Grade 11 learners composed of three strands: 150 STEM learners, 90 HUMSS learners, and 60 GAS learners, the researcher needs a sample of 60 learners. To make sure each strand is fairly represented, the researcher uses stratified sampling. First, the proportion of each group is determined: STEM is 150/300 = 50%, HUMSS is 90/300 = 30%, and GAS is 60/300 = 20%. Next, these same proportions are applied to the sample size of 60. Thus, the researcher will select 30 STEM learners (50% of 60), 18 HUMSS learners (30% of 60), and 12 GAS learners (20% of 60).

-----

Finally, the learners are chosen until the required number of learners from each strand is then selected randomly.

Population Sample

![](img_p75_1.png)

d. Cluster Sampling: The population is divided into

clusters, usually based on location or existing groups (e.g., classes or barangays). A few clusters are randomly selected, and all individuals within those clusters are surveyed.

For example, in a population of 150 Grade 11 learners, the researcher needs a sample of 50 learners. The 150 learners are already divided into 3 sections with 50 learners each. Using cluster sampling, the researcher randomly selects 1 section (cluster) out of the 3. All 50 learners in the chosen section automatically become the sample.

Population Sample

Cluster A is randomly chosen as sample

###### Nonprobability Sampling

Selection is based on convenience or judgment, not chance. Examples include: a. Convenience Sampling

Choosing whoever is easiest to access (e.g., the first 50 learners who enter the cafeteria)

b. Purposive (Judgmental) Sampling

Choosing individuals who possess specific characteristics relevant to the study.

Example: Interviewing only science teachers when studying teaching strategies in science. c. Quota Sampling

The population is divided into groups, and a predetermined number of participants is selected from each group, though the selection is nonrandom.

Example: Surveying 20 male and 20 female learners to reflect gender balance. d. Snowball Sampling

This technique relies on existing participants to recruit additional participants, creating a referral chain.

Example: A learner respondent recommends friends, who in turn recommend others, to join the study. e. Voluntary Response Sampling

In this type, individuals volunteer or decide by themselves to participate in the research.

Example: Learners who willingly respond to an online survey posted by the school.

###### Parameter vs. Statistic

These two terms describe similar measurements but at different levels:

**Parameter: A value that describes a characteristic of the**

entire population (e.g., the average snack preference of all 500 learners). This is usually unknown because it's hard to collect data from everyone.

**Statistic: A value that describes the same characteristic**

but based on the sample (e.g., the average snack preference of the 50 learners surveyed). Statistics are used to estimate parameters. The goal of sampling is to use statistics to make reasonable guesses about parameters.
