###### A. Negation Negation is a logical operation that reverses the truth

value of a given statement. The negation of a statement p is written as ~p), where the symbol ~ means "not."

###### Property of Negation

- If p is true, then ~p is false.
- If p is false, then ~p is true.

| T | F |

###### Truth Value of Conditional Propositions

A conditional proposition may be true or false. To show that a conditional proposition is false, you only need to find one example (called a counter example) in which the hypothesis is fulfilled, and the conclusion is not fulfilled. For example, in the conditional statement: If the *weather is good, then I will play outside, it can be* interpreted as false when the weather is good, but I

**will not play outside.**

The valuation of a conditional proposition can be described using a truth table.

→ T T T

-----

| T | F | F |
|---|---|---|
| F | T | T |
| F | F | T |

Example: Suppose that Mark is a Grade 12 student. Consider this conditional proposition:

Proposition: If Mark is in Grade 12, then he is a Senior High School student. ( → )

What is the truth value of the proposition?

Solution: "Mark is in Grade 12" and "Mark is a senior high school student" are both true because Grade 12 is part of senior high school. Since both the hypothesis and conclusion are true, then the proposition is TRUE, based on the first row of the truth table.

What is the converse, inverse, and contrapositive of the conditional proposition? Express in words and symbols.

Solution:

1. Converse: If Mark is a senior high school student, then he is in Grade 12. ( → )
2. Inverse: If Mark is not in Grade 12, then he is not a senior high school student. (~ → ~)
3. Contrapositive: If Mark is not a senior high school

student, then he is not in Grade 12. (~ → ~)

What is the truth value of each form of the conditional statement?

-----

Solution:

1. Converse: Both the hypothesis and conclusion are true, so the converse of the conditional proposition is also TRUE.
2. Inverse: "Mark is not in Grade 12" and "Mark is not a senior high school" are both false since it was stated that he is in Grade 12. Since both the hypothesis and conclusion is false, the inverse is TRUE as shown in the fourth row of the truth table.
3. Contrapositive: "Mark is not a senior high school" and "Mark is not in Grade 12" are both false since it was stated that he is in Grade 12. Since both the hypothesis and conclusion are false, the contrapositive is TRUE as shown in the fourth row of the truth table.

A tautology is a propositional form that is always true regardless of the truth values of the individual statements substituted for its propositional variables. The word tautology is derived from a Greek word where 'tauto' means 'same' and 'logy' means 'logic'.

###### Strategy 1: Guided Discovery (Inquiry-Based Approach)

**Objective: Help students identify and explain whether**

each statement follows Modus Ponens or Modus Tollens through step-by-step reasoning.

###### Procedure:

1. Begin the activity by recalling the two reasoning patterns:
      - Modus Ponens: If P → Q; P; ∴ Q
      - Modus Tollens: If P → Q; ~Q; ∴ ~P
2. Ask students to read each scenario aloud and

**analyze it.**

3. Guide them with questions such as:
      - "What is the condition (If...)? What is the result (Then...)? "
      - "Was the condition affirmed or denied?"
4. Encourage students to write the conclusion and name the type of reasoning used.
5. Facilitate a short class discussion for each item to confirm answers and clarify misconceptions.

###### Teacher's Note:

Encourage learners to explain their reasoning in their own words before revealing the logical pattern. This deepens understanding rather than rote memorization.

After the performance, you will analyze the arguments by making inferences and explaining your reasoning. You will also evaluate whether the arguments are valid, sound, or fallacious. This activity will help you see how logic is used in everyday life to make clear decisions and avoid faulty conclusions.

## G - Goal

Your goal is to demonstrate how logic operates in real life by performing and analyzing a short scenario that involves reasoning and persuasion. You will show how to make inferences and evaluate arguments as valid, sound, or fallacious, applying logical principles to real-world contexts such as politics, business, sports, or social media.

## R - Role

You are both a role-player and a logic analyst. As a performer, you will act out a realistic situation involving arguments or persuasion. As an analyst, you will step out of character afterward to explain and evaluate the reasoning used in your role-play.

## A - Audience

Your audience will be your classmates and teacher, who will assess how effectively you apply logical reasoning in your performance and post-analysis discussion.

## S - Situation

Everyday life is filled with reasoning-some sound, others misleading. Whether in debates, advertisements, sports commentaries, business meetings, or social media discussions,

arguments are used to persuade or justify opinions. Your group must present a short 2-3 minute role play that depicts one of these real-life situations and then analyze it to

## determine the validity and soundness of the reasoning involved.

## P - Product/Performance

You will present a Role-Play and Analysis in four parts:

1. Role-Play (2-3 minutes). Present a short dramatization of a real-world scenario that includes one or more arguments or persuasive exchanges.

###### Scenario:

*The situation centers on a piece of misleading or fake news that spreads among students, friends, or online users. The characters react differently-some believe and share the information right away, while others question its truth and use logical reasoning and fact-checking to verify it. Through your performance, show how false assumptions, hasty generalizations, or emotional appeals can lead people to accept misinformation, and how applying logical propositions and evidence-based reasoning helps uncover the truth and prevent the spread of fake news.*

2. Inference-Making. After the role play, identify and explain at least two logical inferences that can be drawn from your scenario, supported with reasoning.
3. Argument Evaluation. Identify at least two arguments from your role play and evaluate each as valid, sound, or fallacious, explaining why.

-----

4. Processing & Reflection. Share your group's insights on how logic helps in making

clear decisions, resolving conflicts, and avoiding faulty conclusions in real-life situations.
